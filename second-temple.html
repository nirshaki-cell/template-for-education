Jerusalem · Herod's Temple · about 20 BCE to 70 CE

Start outside the walls and walk inwards, one gate at a time. Each court was holier than the last, and fewer people were allowed in. Select a numbered label to read about it. Double-click the blue gate to go through.

      How the Temple functioned

      The Temple was the one place where Jews offered sacrifice. It worked every day of the year, to a fixed routine, with a large trained staff.

      A day in the Temple

      Before dawn the priests on duty cast lots to share out the tasks. They cleared the ashes from the altar, trimmed the lamps and offered the first lamb of the day with flour, oil and wine. A priest burned incense in the Holy Place, the priests blessed the people, and the Levites sang the psalm for that day.

      Through the day individuals brought their own offerings. In the afternoon a second lamb was offered. These two daily lambs were called the tamid, the continual offering (Exodus 29:38–42; Numbers 28:1–8; Mishnah Tamid).

      Money

      Every adult Jewish man paid a half-shekel each year for the upkeep of the Temple and its public sacrifices (Exodus 30:11–16; Matthew 17:24–27). It had to be paid in approved silver coin, which is why money-changers set up tables (Mishnah Shekalim 1:3).

      Gifts went into thirteen chests in the Court of the Women. The Temple also stored wealth, employed many workers and brought pilgrims' money into Jerusalem.

      Purity

      Worshippers washed by full immersion before going up. Many ritual baths have been excavated near the southern steps. The closer a person came to the centre, the stricter the rules became. The Mishnah lists ten rising degrees of holiness, ending in the Holy of Holies (Kelim 1:6–9).

      Religious and theological significance

      The place of God's presence

      The Temple was understood as the place where God chose to dwell among Israel. Solomon's prayer holds two ideas together: heaven itself cannot contain God, yet God's name is in this house and prayer is heard there (1 Kings 8:27–30). See also Psalm 132:13–14 and Ezekiel 43:1–7.

      Holiness built into space

      The plan of the building teaches a belief. God is holy, so approach is ordered and careful. Each step inwards meant a higher court, richer materials and fewer people. The Holy of Holies was empty: Israel's God was not shown by any image.

      Atonement and forgiveness

      Sacrifice was the God-given way to deal with sin and impurity and to restore the relationship with God (Leviticus 17:11). The Day of Atonement cleansed the sanctuary and the whole people once a year (Leviticus 16).

      Covenant and identity

      One God, one people, one Temple (Deuteronomy 12:5–14). Pilgrimage and the half-shekel tied Jews across the Roman world to Jerusalem. The Temple was also the national centre: court, treasury and symbol of the nation under Roman rule.

      Hope and criticism

      Prophets pictured all nations coming to the Temple (Isaiah 2:2–4; 56:7) and a renewed Temple in the age to come (Ezekiel 40–48). Prophets also warned that worship without justice would not protect the nation (Jeremiah 7:1–15; Amos 5:21–24).

      After 70 CE

      When Rome destroyed the Temple, sacrifice stopped. Rabbinic Judaism centred on prayer, the study of Torah and acts of loving kindness, which a rabbinic tradition says atone as the altar once did (Avot de-Rabbi Natan 4, citing Hosea 6:6). Christians came to speak of Jesus and the Church as the true temple.

      Key dates

      • c. 516 BCEThe Second Temple is completed after the return from exile in Babylon (Ezra 6:15).
      • 167 BCEAntiochus IV desecrates the Temple (1 Maccabees 1).
      • 164 BCEThe Maccabees rededicate it. This is the origin of Hanukkah (1 Maccabees 4:36–59).
      • 63 BCEThe Roman general Pompey captures Jerusalem and enters the sanctuary.
      • c. 20/19 BCEHerod the Great begins rebuilding on a much larger scale (Josephus, Antiquities 15.380).
      • c. 62–64 CEWork on the whole complex finally ends (Antiquities 20.219). Compare John 2:20.
      • 70 CEThe Roman army under Titus destroys the Temple at the end of the Jewish revolt.

      About the pictures and the sources

      The pictures are simplified drawings, not photographs. No image of the Temple survives from the time. Shapes and positions follow the ancient descriptions, but sizes are approximate and some details are guesses shared by most reconstructions.

      The two main sources sometimes disagree. Josephus was a priest who saw the Temple before 70 CE (Jewish War 5.184–247; Antiquities 15.380–425). The Mishnah tractate Middot was written down around 200 CE from earlier memory. Where they differ, the labels say so.

      Some locations are uncertain. This applies to the Beautiful Gate, the traders' tables and the Chamber of Hewn Stone. The labels flag each one.

      Biblical wording follows the NRSV. A cubit was roughly half a metre.
