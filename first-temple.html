      How the Temple functioned

      The first Temple stood for almost four hundred years. The Bible tells us less about its daily routine than about its building. Much of the detail below comes from the Law (Leviticus) and from Chronicles, which was written after the exile and may describe some later practice.

      Daily worship

      A burnt offering was made every morning and a grain offering every evening (2 Kings 16:15). Extra offerings were made on Sabbaths and new moons. Incense was burned and the lamps were kept alight.

      Chronicles describes Levites singing with cymbals, harps and lyres, and priests blowing trumpets (2 Chronicles 5:12–13). Many of the Psalms were probably sung here.

      The king's Temple

      The king built the Temple, paid for it and was responsible for its repair. Solomon himself prays, blesses the people and offers sacrifice at the dedication (1 Kings 8). Kings were crowned in the Temple (2 Kings 11:12).

      A king could reform the worship, as Hezekiah and Josiah did, or corrupt it, as Ahaz and Manasseh did (2 Kings 16; 21).

      Festivals

      Three times a year the people came up for the pilgrim festivals: Passover with Unleavened Bread, Weeks and Tabernacles (Deuteronomy 16:16; 1 Kings 9:25). The Temple was dedicated at the autumn festival (1 Kings 8:2). Josiah's Passover is singled out as the greatest (2 Kings 23:21–23).

      Treasury and upkeep

      The Temple held the nation's treasure (1 Kings 7:51). King Joash set a chest beside the altar to collect money for repairs (2 Kings 12:9–15). It was during repairs under Josiah that the book of the law was found (2 Kings 22:3–10).

      Religious and theological significance

      God's dwelling: name and glory

      The cloud of glory fills the house, showing that God is present (1 Kings 8:10–11). Yet Solomon asks, will God really dwell on earth? Heaven cannot contain him. So the Temple is the place where God's name is, and towards which people pray (1 Kings 8:27–30). Presence and transcendence are held together.

      A throne room

      God is "enthroned on the cherubim" (Psalm 99:1) and the Ark is his footstool. There was no statue of God. The throne was empty to the eye. This set Israel's Temple apart from the temples of its neighbours, whose plan it otherwise shared.

      Zion and the house of David

      God promises David a lasting dynasty, and David's son builds God's house (2 Samuel 7:12–13). Temple and kingship support each other. The psalms of Zion celebrate Jerusalem as the city God has chosen and defends (Psalms 46; 48; 132).

      Covenant and law

      At the centre of the building lay the tablets of the commandments. Worship was tied to the covenant made at Sinai. Deuteronomy calls for one place of sacrifice (Deuteronomy 12:5–14), and Josiah's reform closed the other shrines (2 Kings 23).

      A house of prayer

      Solomon's prayer pictures people turning towards the Temple in drought, defeat, sickness and sin, and being forgiven. It includes the foreigner who comes from a distant land (1 Kings 8:41–43).

      The prophets' warning

      Jeremiah stood in the gate and told the people not to trust in the words "the temple of the Lord". Without justice the building would not save them. God would treat it as he had treated the old shrine at Shiloh (Jeremiah 7:1–15).

      Destruction and exile

      In 587/586 BCE the Babylonian army burned the Temple and carried its bronze and gold away (2 Kings 25:8–17). This was a crisis of faith: had God been defeated, or had God judged his own people? See Psalm 137 and Lamentations. Ezekiel answers that the glory left before the Temple fell, and promises its return (Ezekiel 10–11; 43:1–5).

      Questions to think about

      1. Name the three parts of the Temple building, from the entrance inwards.
      2. What stood in the Holy of Holies, and what did each object mean?
      3. In 1 Kings 8:27–30, how does Solomon hold together the belief that God is present in the Temple and the belief that God cannot be contained?
      4. What does the position of the Temple beside the palace suggest about the link between religion and kingship?
      5. "The destruction of the first Temple strengthened Israel's faith more than it damaged it." How far do you agree? Use Jeremiah 7:1–15 and Ezekiel 10–11.

      About the pictures and the sources

      The pictures are simplified drawings. Nothing of Solomon's Temple has been found, and the site cannot be excavated. Every reconstruction is built from the biblical text.

      The main sources are 1 Kings 5–8 and 2 Chronicles 2–7. Chronicles was written later and sometimes gives different figures. Examples are the height of the pillars (18 or 35 cubits) and the capacity of the Sea (2,000 or 3,000 baths). The labels say where they differ.

      Some things are guesses. The Bible does not give the layout of the palace buildings, the size of the courts, the shape of the altar or the position of the table. The labels flag each one. Ezekiel 40–48 describes a temple seen in a vision, so it is not used here as a description of Solomon's building.

      Comparisons help. Temples of the same period at Ain Dara and Tell Tayinat in Syria have a similar three-part plan.

      Biblical wording follows the NRSV. A cubit was roughly half a metre.
